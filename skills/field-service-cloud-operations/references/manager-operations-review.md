## Non-negotiable Manager Operations Review objective

The Manager Operations Review is an internal account-management report for one manager and an exact inclusive period of at most 31 days. Use it for any explicit actual-versus-plan review, even one day, and for weekly, semi-monthly, calendar-month, rolling, or other custom multi-day internal management reviews. Lead with four decisions: **Which customers can I update, and which updates need confirmation first? Which property problems or commitments need follow-up now? What meaningful completed work can I communicate? Which established dates are coming next?** Then explain whether planned service was delivered and where credible time or planning comparisons need attention. Keep Operations Brief's daily crew handoff, intentionally dated Morning Brief, one-jobsite Operations Summary and customer-facing Monthly Client Report as separate routes.

Use only the returned manager scope, exact period, server-computed metrics, exact collection counts, availability states, and derived outlook. Preserve server-computed coverage percentages, duration variance, prior-period changes, labor totals and trend rather than recalculating them from displayed rows. Counting complete matching records or adding complete, nonoverlapping stop-count rows into a crew subtotal is different from inventing a new performance measure; the data guide explains those groupings. Scheduled service and recorded service are different facts. A task assignment is not performance evidence. A reported concern is not a proven incident, cause, severity, callback, or inspected photo. List each text-supported safety concern individually and state when partial or truncated task evidence prevents a complete period-wide classification.

The quality objective is a report a landscape maintenance account manager can act on without reconstructing the work from counts or generic task names. Use the returned manager/crew scope; do not claim it is a separately verified book of assigned customer accounts. Company-specific task procedures and preferences belong in customization, not universal rules.

## Build and review the report

Write a useful management review from the returned records. Explain what happened during the requested period, what needs attention now, and what was scheduled in the following period. Keep those timeframes distinct.

### Current follow-up when the report is requested

For a new review or explicit refresh, retrieve fresh MOR records for the exact requested period. Show that period and the returned `currentState.stateDate` for current saved work. A state date is not an exact capture time or an atomic snapshot; never invent an as-of timestamp. Current open tasks remain current follow-up even when the review period is historical.

Use existing returned evidence when it answers the four decisions. If current customer/completed-work evidence or future dates are missing because the requested period and its following-period outlook are historical, use the same MOR tool with the same optional human manager selector for `dateFrom=stateDate-6` and `dateTo=stateDate`. Its existing weekly calculation supplies recent work and the next seven calendar dates. Keep that supplemental window separate from the requested period and retain the user's explicit outlook. Necessary MOR paging and exact-task detail retain their existing scope and stopping rules. Do not use Operations Brief, silently replace the requested period, merge independent portfolios, or forward a Morning Brief-only `All` alias. Compare returned manager scope and current-state date before combining findings; a mismatch is a specific limitation, not permission to choose another scope or manufacture a common snapshot.

Current communication candidates require a saved current Complete status, a genuine completion entry in the relevant window and enough work detail to say what was finished. MOR completion collections can include Closed or reopened tasks; preserve their historical facts, but confirm reopened or uncertain work before recommending a completion update. An eligible candidate does not establish that the customer has never been updated. Name the customer or contact only when returned; otherwise identify the property and the customer update to consider, without inventing a contact.

For upcoming work, require an explicit returned due date or planned service date after the current-state date and within the stated outlook. Describe a due date as a deadline and a route-derived date as a maintenance opportunity, not a booked task appointment. Past following-period dates remain historical planning evidence. Do not infer a future schedule from recurrence, an undated promise or an old task title. Distinguish no qualifying returned dates from unavailable or incomplete outlook detail.

Workspace-reviewed selections, source keys, office wording, photos and captions take precedence in the existing draft/refine/export branch. Do not add supplemental or unselected current records to a reviewed report or silently refresh it during file generation. A new review or an explicit refresh is a separate action; retain the selected facts and edits for ordinary refinement/export.

Before selecting report content, review every returned task's title or subject, description and description history, attachment descriptions or captions, individual checklist items and their completion states, and `workOrderLines`, wherever those fields are provided. Interpret that combined context with the task's status, dates and assignment, including tasks with blank descriptions. Do not use title, category or status filters to decide which tasks receive this review, or replace substantive fields with counts alone. Select, group or omit findings only after evaluating their content. Preserve distinctions that change the manager's next action.

When a large tool result is saved to a file, read it in manageable task batches while preserving each task's substantive fields. Reduce the number of tasks per batch rather than cutting their text or projecting content into counts. Include checklist notes and both sectioned and unsectioned work-order items. A blank current description does not mean the task lacks an explanation: read its saved description updates and other nonempty content before making that claim. Review completed tasks independently of customer-update candidates; those collections overlap but customer updates can omit completed Extras.

Task records use shared content previews with exact counts. Empty available sections need no follow-up. If omitted history, checklist work, attachment descriptions or work-order lines are needed to understand a finding or required action, retrieve the exact task's full detail automatically; do not ask the user for separate permission or treat omitted content as absent. Exact-task retrieval is independent of the report dates. For get_task_data, use mode=work_items, taskId and one returned authorized scope ID without report dates. If only a property name is available, resolve it through get_property_data first; use a unique match, never guess an ID or choose among ambiguous matches. If scope cannot be resolved, ask for the necessary clarification and identify the detail still unavailable. Inspect relevant images using the returned attachment IDs.

Use `openTasks.olderSummary` to summarize stale field-open backlog by task age, type and current status rather than listing every old task. Stale means the latest recorded update is before `updatedBefore`, three calendar months before `currentState.stateDate`; age alone does not make a task stale. Task age and age bands still start at the earliest temporal `effectiveDate` (`firstEffectiveLocal`), never the latest update. The active backlog total equals the individual-task count plus `olderSummary.activeCount`; neither is additional to the total. Separately, `completeCount` and Complete groups preserve older crew-finished tasks awaiting manager closure, including recently updated tasks; do not describe those as necessarily stale. Company labels may differ from Complete. Keep Complete distinct from Closed and completion dates distinct from closure dates. Recommend reviewing stale crew tasks to determine whether the work was already done, is no longer needed, or remains to be scheduled or reassigned. Use each returned `reviewUrl`, explaining that it opens the broader task list sorted oldest first, not an exact inactivity/age/type/status filter. Do not construct unsupported filters. Preserve individually returned period work and upcoming tasks. Retrieve details when needed for a specific task or a conclusion beyond that backlog-review recommendation.

Age/type/status groupings do not establish task assignment or suitability for a Maintenance visit. Keep older tasks as an unassessed backlog unless their details support including or excluding them from next-visit work. A recurring route date is a plan; it does not prove a crew is onsite, has attended, or will complete a task.

Organize related tasks into useful conversations, but keep each task's facts attached to that task. A shared property or topic does not establish a shared cause, duplicate relationship or resolution. If their relationship is uncertain, frame it as something to confirm.

Use the server's totals and calculated comparisons within their stated scope. Missing scheduled-day records support asking what happened that day; broader absence claims require broader evidence. Credible planned-time comparisons at selected jobsites remain useful even when plans elsewhere are missing.

Give the manager a compact property-grouped action plan before supporting service and period detail. Every included attention item needs the actual concern, recorded status, current assignee or an explicit missing-assignee fact, relevant date and concrete next check. Use property subheadings with short issue bullets and a bold next-check line; keep the facts and action adjacent so both fit the chat's normal reading width. Do not put multi-sentence concerns and actions in side-by-side table columns. Supporting numeric comparisons may use concise tables, but the action plan must not require horizontal scrolling to find whom to contact or what to ask. Group supporting crew context rather than making the manager select or read every routine visit. Preserve distinct consequential concerns. Original and supplemental collections overlap: keep one current action per task, retain a distinct historical accomplishment when needed, and never add totals from different windows.

Before delivery, read the report as the account manager who has just run it. Can the reader answer all four decisions, identify the property and person to contact, understand what was reported or finished, and make the next check from the report itself? A generic "needs review" label, a count-only section or an overdue date without the actual work does not pass. Give a concise answer when a question has no supported candidates, distinguishing an available empty result from filtered, summarized, partial or unavailable evidence; do not imply the whole portfolio is clear. Correct unsupported connections, dates, counts and assurances. This is the writer's source-to-report pass; no separate reader, review task or saved working draft is required.

### Read the business facts

The result describes scheduled work, recorded visits and task records, with different dates and units. Establish those meanings before deciding how the month went. The report sections below organize the conclusions; they are not categories into which every record needs to fit.

| Management question | Relevant evidence | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| What happened during this review? | `period`, visit `evidenceDate`, and the dated completed-work records | `currentState.stateDate` instead dates the current backlog, assignments and aging. `outlook` is the window after the review, which may already be in the past. |
| Was this open task created during the review? | Its `createdLocal` date compared with `period` | `ageDays` and `ageBucket` measure age at `currentState.stateDate`, not at the review's end. An old task is not necessarily unfinished field work or a record to discard. |
| Was service recorded on the assigned day? | `serviceCoverage` counts for the scheduled crew, property and date | A scheduled-day exception is not proof of missed work. `completedWork.recordedVisits` is the full visit history; `unscheduledRecordedVisits` is only its off-schedule subset. |
| How long was a particular visit? | The visit's `actualDurationMinutes` | This is recorded on-site time. Null means unavailable, not zero. It is not crew-hours or man-hours. |
| Can we report aggregate labor? | `labor` totals, basis, reason and `coveredVisitCount` / `eligibleVisitCount` | `eligibleVisitCount` is the recorded-visit denominator; `coveredVisitCount` counts visits with individual man-hour evidence, not visits with on-site minutes. Aggregate labor can still be unavailable: its returned basis, reason and availability determine what can be reported. Neither unavailable allocation nor these counts establishes a company-wide timekeeping problem. |
| Can we compare time with the plan? | Credible saved `plannedDurationMinutes` and returned comparisons for the named jobsites; missing or invalid plans elsewhere limit broader conclusions | Calculated variance does not validate the plan. Compare usable plans within their stated scope; report only actuals where plans are missing or invalid. Selected comparisons do not establish manager-wide productivity. |
| What needs a manager's attention? | Each task's subject, description, individual checklist states and attachment descriptions/labels, interpreted together | Captions are saved work context even when the task description is empty or its title is routine. Keep every distinct material safety, property, access, water or customer concern; saved text reports a condition, not proof of today's condition. |
| What was completed, and whom should we contact? | Saved task status and each checklist item's state; current `assignedCrew` for follow-up | An open task can contain completed steps. Assignment is not proof of who did the work. A status, age or custom task title alone does not prove neglect, urgency, compliance or required cleanup. |

Read the complete returned task context before selecting priorities, including ordinary Assigned tasks; selection is an editorial decision after understanding the work, not a reason to inspect only urgent-looking titles or statuses. For a conclusion about a property's service pattern, use its complete matching visit history or limit the statement to the scheduled-day question. Preserve exact collection totals and omissions.

## Manager Operations Review

Begin with the returned manager scope, requested period and current saved-state date, then an **Account-manager action plan** grouped by property. Answer these four questions even when a concise evidence-qualified empty answer is appropriate:

- **Customer updates:** Identify verified work ready to communicate and customer requests needing a status check first. State what the customer can be told from the records and whom the manager should ask when confirmation is needed. Do not claim a message is overdue or unsent without saved communication evidence.
- **Property follow-up:** Prioritize consequential safety, property, customer and commitment concerns. Explain the actual concern, status, assignment, relevant date and next check. A saved deadline supports overdue wording; an old unchecked step or a passed route opportunity alone does not. Group by property while retaining separate task facts and assignees. Preserve the returned open total and distinguish selected priorities from summarized or unassessed older work.
- **Work to communicate:** Select meaningful accomplishments, improvements and completed customer requests from the requested period or the explicitly labelled recent window. Summarize routine maintenance; do not list every visit. Keep each accomplishment in its actual window and avoid repeating a customer-update task already explained above.
- **Coming next:** Give established future deadlines, planned maintenance opportunities and explicit commitments under the actual dated outlook. Include the property, work and responsible person when available. Separate work needing a date from work with an established date; historical following-period dates do not belong here.

Then provide **Review-period results**: an overall assessment, scheduled versus recorded service, credible time comparisons, returned prior-period changes and the few crew details needed to understand those results. Keep server totals within their stated period. A historical following-period outlook may be included here under its actual dates when it explains the period's planning. Use **Records to improve** only for limitations that affect a decision and a proportionate next step; a missing visit, task age or blank description alone does not diagnose poor work, faulty phones or a recordkeeping problem. Scale supporting detail to the requested review and keep it expandable in the workspace where existing controls support that behavior.

Keep task numbers when they help the manager locate the work. Use returned people's names or plain-language work groups instead of internal crew/route codes. Remove an internal identifier prefix from a property label; preserve a street number only when separate address evidence confirms it. Provide routine supporting detail when requested or needed to substantiate a conclusion.

### Understand what the review describes

The result combines three views of the business: the route schedule, recorded activity, and task records. They answer different questions and overlap; they are not three piles of completed work to add together. Explain the recorded fact, what it suggests, and the management decision without collapsing them into one claim. This applies equally to a short heading and its supporting paragraph: a careful explanation cannot repair a heading that says work was missed or never done. Use the returned full-period totals to establish the overall picture, then use the detail to explain the few management decisions that matter. When the records leave the outcome uncertain, organize the discussion by crew, property or work issue rather than assigning an inferred outcome. For example, several recorded visits on different dates can support asking whether service days moved; they need not become a counted group of rescheduled stops or a claim that every other stop was missed. Useful interpretation remains welcome when its basis and uncertainty are clear.

Keep later-created tasks in current follow-up, clearly dated, rather than the month's accomplishments or problems. Later creation or reassignment does not remove a material concern from current follow-up. Describe a historical outlook as what was scheduled then, not preparations the manager can still make.

**Which records describe all visits, and which describe exceptions?** `serviceCoverage.totals`, `serviceCoverage.crewDates` and `serviceCoverage.jobsites` summarize the same scheduled stops, including successful matches. `serviceCoverage.stopExceptions` contains only scheduled stops without a confirmed successful match. `serviceCoverage.unscheduledRecordedVisits` contains visits outside the exact crew/property/date schedule. Combining those two exception lists still leaves out successful scheduled visits: neither list is a property's full activity history. A positive `servicedStopCount` for the property or `matchedVisitCount` in its returned time comparison already establishes that visits were recorded, even when the exception lists show no other visits. Those counts can rule out “no visits” without another call; they do not explain what happened on the unmatched dates. `actualDurationVariance.stopExceptions` is a different subset: matched visits selected for a calculated time exception. For full visit history, use `completedWork.recordedVisits`, which includes incomplete or uncertain visits as well as completed ones despite its parent name. “Unscheduled” means no corresponding crew/property/date schedule, not that the property is absent from every route.

**How the schedule and recorded visits relate.** A scheduled stop is an expectation: a named crew at a property on a date. A recorded visit is an observation with its own crew, property, date and service state. The scheduled-day measure links a stop to qualifying service only when crew, property and date agree; it measures how closely recorded service followed the route, not whether all work at the property was delivered. An unmatched stop can justify asking the assigned crew leader what happened without first investigating every other crew or date. Keep that question scoped to the scheduled day. When a broader conclusion depends on other visits, read the property's matching history, including visits that already matched other scheduled days. Those visits remain relevant activity without satisfying a second stop. Describe the relevant dates, crews and service states, then the question the manager needs to resolve. “Probably moved” and recurring route-pattern conclusions are useful when the dates, crews and schedule favor that explanation; explain that basis while keeping the server's match counts unchanged. Complete history can establish that no visit was recorded for stated crews and dates, but not that no work occurred. Build group descriptions and headings from the same member facts as their paragraphs, so the heading does not assert an absence or cause that those facts contradict.

The returned `servicedStopCount` and `coveragePercent` measure assigned-crew, scheduled-day matches, not overall delivery at the property. Preserve the exact counts, percentages and comparison changes. Present the nonzero unmatched-visit total alongside them: “The crews had [scheduled] scheduled stops; [serviced] have completed visits recorded on the assigned day. Another [unmatched visits] visits were recorded outside that exact crew/day schedule.” Never add unmatched visits to serviced stops or compute adjusted coverage. Recorded-visit counts measure activity volume; task-completion counts measure task records, not more visits.

**What does a service record establish?** `serviced` means a completed recorded interval with positive actual service duration. It does not certify work quality or completion of every task. `partial_or_unproven` means the visit record needs review; it does not establish that work was partially completed or missed; `unknown` means evidence is insufficient or conflicting. A null `actualDurationMinutes` means time is unavailable, not a zero-minute visit. These fields do not explain whether a phone setting, GPS collection or manual check-in caused an incomplete record. The useful question is what work occurred and whether the service record needs correction. Changing the route can correct schedule expectations; it cannot repair missing service evidence.

**How do the records connect?** Visit rows expose `propertyName`, `crewName`, `crewLeader` and `evidenceDate`; scheduled rows use `serviceDate`. Preserve the full returned property and crew names while matching or grouping, even when codes or prefixes are omitted from the final prose. Do not invent absent property or crew IDs, merge similar names, or substitute the scheduled crew for the crew on the visit. A shared property, topic or next conversation is a useful way to organize a review, but it is not evidence that the grouped records share work details. A description, checklist step, estimate or reported condition belongs to its own task; a visit's crew and date belong to that visit. Keep those relationships when shortening the prose. Before giving named records or properties one shared description, test the stated condition against each member's own supporting facts. Split or narrow the description when a member does not support it: no off-schedule visit explaining an unmatched stop is not the same condition as no recorded visit at the property. State the shared management question once, then attach differing facts to the task or property that supports them. A count or all/none/only statement about a collection requires evaluating that condition across its complete matching records, not just the concerns selected for discussion. When only a subset was examined, name that subset or omit the collection-wide claim; a reported collection total does not make a selected-record calculation complete. For a per-crew stop subtotal, group one complete rollup (`crewDates` or `jobsites`) by crew and add its returned stop counts; never combine the two overlapping rollups or divide the overall total among crews. Representative examples can stay brief without being a partition of every record.

Each collection's `count` and `page` describe its own scope. Complete unscheduled detail is still only that subset. Retrieve `completedWork.recordedVisits` when a conclusion depends on the property's visit history; null count/items/page means that detail was not requested. A claim of no recorded visit requires complete matching history for the stated crews and dates. When it is unavailable, describe the scheduled-day question, not an absence of all service. Use a detail response to fill its requested collection; unrelated null sections do not erase earlier results. Collections overlap, so do not concatenate their rows and count the same activity again. Preserve omitted counts, and do not present a partial page as a complete classification. A few examples support “several,” not “most” or “only.”

Recurring off-day service can justify a route review when complete crew/property/date detail shows a repeated pattern and both the current and comparison periods have poor scheduled-day adherence. Ask whether that pattern is intentional; if confirmed, recommend aligning the route with actual practice. Low percentages alone support reconciliation, not a route-change diagnosis. Recommendations remain advisory.

**What needs follow-up?** Completed tasks, customer-update candidates, open tasks and upcoming tasks are overlapping views of task records, not separate batches of work. Use the task number to keep one coherent work item. `status` describes the saved workflow stage, not an independent observation of field conditions. Customer Approved means approval is recorded; an open approved task needs its completion or remaining work confirmed. Reported means a concern was entered, not that nobody answered it. In progress means that stage is saved, not that work has continued since the creation date. Use headings such as **Approved work needing a status update**, and ask the current assignee what remains or whether the record should be closed before requesting a completion date. A note saying to close, remove or merge a task makes it record cleanup rather than confirmed outstanding field work; retain it in the saved status count while explaining the different follow-up. Assignment identifies whom to contact, not who performed the work.

Within an open task, some checklist steps can already be complete. Each item's completion and required flags identify its recorded progress; summary counts show how much remains but not which work is unfinished. Use the individual item text and state to explain the next decision. Read subject, description, checklist and attachment/photo labels together as the saved work context: an empty description field does not make the task undescribed or unusable when another field explains it. Keep the review selective, but carry forward each distinct reported condition whose saved context supports a material safety, property, access, water or customer decision. Explain what was reported, its date, the current assignee and what needs confirming; a reported risk is not proven damage or proof of today's condition. Material concerns can appear in an ordinary inspection caption rather than an urgent title: falling material or soil washing toward an access area can justify follow-up without inventing an incident. Group routine tasks and omit captions that add no decision-relevant context, but do not use that compression to hide a distinct material concern. A stressed plant alone does not establish a hazard; describe only the condition and decision the text supports.

Keep the returned open-task total intact, including tasks originating with the manager's maintenance crews that are now assigned to irrigation, enhancement or other crews. Name the current assignee for follow-up. `dueState` concerns an explicit due date; `maintenanceExpectationState` concerns a route-derived opportunity to address the task. An expected visit having passed does not turn every undated task into an overdue deadline or prove the task was not done. Preserve the separate returned totals and dates. The review should help the manager decide which crew conversation, customer update or record correction is needed.

Include contracted work only when requested or when a nonzero result or exception changes a decision; call it **contracted work completed** and describe the service. Omit an irrelevant zero count. Unavailable aggregate labor cannot be reconstructed by adding overlapping visit times or multiplying by today's crew size.

Use scheduled stops lacking qualifying service evidence, partial or unknown coverage, customer-update candidates, open work whose expected maintenance visit has passed, overdue or aging tasks, and text-supported safety concerns, customer complaints, possible callbacks, or equipment issues to select the property-grouped **Account-manager action plan**. Cite the task number and saved supporting text for included work. Preserve saved status and assignment. Enhancement-related tasks are recorded work status, not a sales pipeline; do not infer approval, value, probability, or billing readiness.

**Can we judge time against the plan?** A returned arithmetic result and a credible operational comparison are not the same thing. `actualDurationMinutes` is recorded on-site time; `plannedDurationMinutes` is saved route planning data. Returned variance and trend describe calculations on eligible records, not validation that the underlying plans are complete or sensible. Duration-exception and comparison-candidate lists omit records that lack usable inputs. Judge the plans against the entire returned or counted manager scope, including eligible visits and jobsites missing from those lists.

Use planned-versus-actual findings for named jobsites whose saved plans are internally consistent and credible as minutes for the work described. Missing plans elsewhere do not invalidate those comparisons. State that the findings cover only those jobsites and use them to support checking the plan or recorded work, not to judge the entire manager scope. Widespread blanks, mixed units or implausible values—such as under ten minutes for an ordinary landscape-maintenance jobsite—prevent a reliable manager-wide comparison. Exclude invalid inputs; do not convert suspicious values into another unit or fill plans with actuals. For jobsites without a usable plan, explain recorded time and useful variation between visits without an over/under-plan judgment. If no credible comparisons remain, make the review actual-only and recommend adding or correcting planned durations. A suspect saved value may illustrate a planning error when clearly identified as unreliable, never as a performance benchmark.

When plans are credible, use the returned computed comparisons, keeping actuals, plans, variance and trend distinct. Use the matched-visit count and work context to judge how much weight a difference deserves; two visits give less support for a recurring pattern than repeated comparable visits. Differing daily plans can reflect different scheduled work, not a planning error. The model decides whether a comparison warrants a crew conversation or plan review; a numerical threshold does not decide that action. Saved planning tolerance is not measured performance. Put credible findings with Review-period results; put evidenced planning errors and their corrective action under Records to improve. Unavailable labor allocation remains unavailable, not an inference about crew productivity.

Use recordkeeping gaps under **Records to improve** only when they affect a decision. Put relevant material or pesticide activity with the work it explains, not in a miscellaneous inventory. Use each actual outlook window for tasks returned in that window; a date on a current-backlog task does not by itself place it in the outlook. Reporting state alone does not create a pesticide follow-up reason. Keep due dates and maintenance expectations distinct; neither is a promised appointment. Consolidate specific evidence-backed actions in the **Account-manager action plan** without inventing owners or commitments.

A page size limits one response, not the review. Follow available pages for the task collections and dated outlook needed to finish the report; merely disclosing a partial page does not replace retrieving an available continuation. There is no fixed MOR call allowance. Keep tenant, manager, period, detail section and limit unchanged for a cursor. Stop on denial, expired or nonadvancing cursors, or repeated failure and disclose what remains unavailable; never combine contradictory pages.

Normal Manager Operations Reviews end in chat. Create an internal PDF only when the user explicitly asks for PDF, an editable Word document only when explicitly asked for Word, and both only when explicitly asked for both. Build every requested artifact from the same accepted structured facts, reconcile its periods, counts, omissions, coverage, duration findings, backlog, and outlook to the chat result, and render and inspect every page. Never generate, send, publish, or share an artifact implicitly.

## Examples of useful and misleading interpretations

These fictional examples illustrate reasoning from the same records, not required wording, report categories or client procedures. The successful versions explain what the facts mean for a crew conversation without deciding more than the records support.

**A recorded status is not assurance about the underlying work.** Pesticide reporting records are marked Submitted, with no follow-up reason returned.

- Misleading: "All pesticide reporting was properly completed and no follow-up is needed." This adds assurance about correctness and remaining obligations that the saved state does not supply.
- Useful: "The pesticide reports are marked submitted." Include a reporting follow-up only when another returned fact supports it; do not invent a problem merely to avoid giving unsupported reassurance. The manager can understand the recorded progress without the review certifying compliance.

**A nearby visit already matched another scheduled day.** Cedar Court was scheduled for Alex's crew on Tuesday and Wednesday. The complete returned history contains a completed Wednesday visit by that crew, matched to Wednesday's stop, and no Tuesday visit.

- Misleading: under **No nearby visits**, “Cedar Court's Tuesday service was missed.” This confuses an unmatched scheduled stop with absence of activity, and then with absence of work.
- Useful: “Cedar Court has Wednesday's service recorded, but no Tuesday visit. Ask Alex whether both days were intended and what happened to Tuesday's work.” Wednesday remains visible without being counted twice. If instead a completed Wednesday visit were off-schedule against a Tuesday-only plan, “Tuesday's service probably moved to Wednesday” could be reasonable, with the crew/date basis and uncertainty stated.

**The count and description refer to the same records.** Five completed visit records cover two properties. Pine Court has Maria's visits on Monday and Thursday and Ben's on Friday. Oak Court has Maria's visits on Monday and Thursday.

- Misleading: “Two visits were completed” counts properties but calls them visits. “Maria's crew completed all five visits” counts the visits correctly but assigns Ben's visit to Maria.
- Useful: “Five visits were recorded at two properties—four by Maria's crew and one by Ben's.” If crew attribution does not affect the decision, “Five visits were recorded at two properties” is sufficient. The number describes the named unit: stops, visits, task records and distinct properties have different counts. Grouping several tasks under one property does not turn them into one task. A summary can omit routine examples, but its stated count still comes from the complete records matching what the sentence says.

**An open task contains work already recorded as complete.** A task assigned to Lee's crew is saved as In progress. Its checklist marks replacing a broken sprinkler complete and preparing an estimate for drainage work incomplete.

- Misleading: “Lee still needs to replace the sprinkler and prepare the drainage estimate.” This treats the open task as if every step remains unfinished and treats assignment as performance evidence.
- Useful: “The sprinkler replacement is checked complete; the drainage estimate remains unchecked. Ask Lee whether the estimate is ready.” This preserves the saved progress without claiming who performed the repair. A custom task title alone would not establish either action.

**Related work is not identical work.** Cedar Court has two inspection tasks and a separate drainage-estimate task. Completed work at Pine Court records mowing and leaf collection; Oak Court records mowing and blowing.

- Misleading: “The Cedar Court inspections need the drainage estimate,” or “Crews completed mowing and leaf collection at Pine Court and Oak Court.” The facts exist, but each sentence attaches one record's detail to other records that do not support it.
- Useful: “At Cedar Court, review the inspection findings and confirm the separate drainage estimate's status. Routine work included mowing and leaf collection at Pine Court, and mowing and blowing at Oak Court.” These facts can share a management discussion without sharing an estimate or every work activity. If the distinction adds no decision value, summarize the two properties as routine grounds maintenance rather than extending the more specific activity to both.
