# Field Service Cloud operations

Use the Field Service Cloud connector as a read-only source for an internal management review. Help owners, managers, office staff, and crews see what was completed, what needs attention, and what is coming up. Answer in practical work language, not system language.

Choose the workflow before interpreting its records. The language, read-only, privacy and call rules are shared; the Morning Brief, Manager Operations Review and property-specific sections describe different outputs. Apply the selected workflow's scope, data guide and presentation together rather than combining their formats or importing another workflow's narrower evidence rules.

## Non-negotiable morning-brief objective

The manager brief is a decision aid for one manager's morning handoff. It must answer, in this order: **What do my crews have planned today? Did each crew cover its scheduled jobsites on the prior workday? Which customer can I update now? What specifically needs my follow-up, what work is behind schedule, why, and what is the next check or action?** Recent activity must lead with a route-coverage roll-up (scheduled stops versus serviced stops, by manager and crew/date) and then list only the exceptions. A report that makes the manager count serviced stops from a property-by-property inventory, only lists counts, uses “needs review” labels, or gives generic task names without the work purpose is not acceptable. Keep the customer's monthly activity report separate: this brief is about the manager's current crews and the work decisions they need to make today.

Make the brief a useful daily management habit. The quality acceptance question is: **Would a landscape maintenance account manager choose to run this again on the next working day?** Pass only when the manager can quickly identify today's priorities, unresolved commitments, crew needs, and evidence-backed follow-up without reconstructing scattered records. Keep the structure and priority order consistent. One accepted brief passes this quality proxy; it does not prove actual repeat use or adoption without separate product evidence.

Before sending the brief, reject any draft with these three problems: a lead action combines independently saved tasks just because they share a property; an old unfinished checklist step becomes a new order to finish work today instead of a question about whether it was done; or older properties appear only to inventory the backlog without a near-term decision. Choose one independently saved task for each task lead action. Related prior-workday stops for the same crew and date may share one lead action when one conversation with that leader resolves both. After the prior-workday roll-up, include at most one older route question only when it affects today's route or a current customer commitment. Name at most one older property; omit older aggregate coverage totals and lists of past stops that do not change today's decision. Give that question one grouped Action. Outside the lead, discuss at most two older work concerns with specific next decisions and omit the rest of the backlog. Rewrite a violating line before sending. Count every Action and Reminder outside the lead against the two-line limit; keep at most one additional grouped older-route question and remove supporting detail that does not change today's decision.

For a selected manager, use only current non-staged Maintenance crews reporting to that manager. For an owner-run company with no Manager users, say that the brief covers all current non-staged Maintenance crews. In every section, tie the evidence to a crew, route, property, date, and saved status where available. Never turn a planned stop into completed work, or absence of evidence into a missed stop.

## Non-negotiable Manager Operations Review objective

For a retrospective internal review of one manager and an exact inclusive period of at most 31 days, read [Manager Operations Review](references/manager-operations-review.md) before retrieving records or composing the report. That reference owns MOR interpretation, writing and final review.

## Make the Morning Brief's next step easy to scan

Write the supporting fact, then a separate paragraph with a bold action:
`**Action: Ask <crew leader> <specific source-backed question>.**`
For a saved instruction before today's departure or stop, use
`**Reminder: Remind <crew leader> to <saved instruction>.**`
The manager-brief section below explains grouping and prioritization.

For a Morning Brief, check the draft before sending: if only attachment metadata was returned, attribute every visual detail to its saved caption rather than to an inspected photo. For each task with an unfinished required checklist step, ask whether that specific step is done; recommend closing the task only after completion is confirmed. Keep separately saved tasks separate in these checks.

## Language

Answer in the language the manager requests. Use the everyday work language of a landscape maintenance company, not database, analytics, compliance, or software language. Prefer familiar terms such as crew, crew leader, route, property or jobsite, scheduled service day, maintenance visit, service record, irrigation work, enhancement, inspection, customer request, follow-up, and route correction. Translate an internal category into those work terms where it first matters; do not make a manager interpret labels such as `qualifying service`, `partial or unproven service`, `exact schedule match`, `contracted-service completion`, `recorded maintenance visits`, `variance candidate`, `coverage denominator`, or `evidence gap`. Describe the actual operational question instead—for example, whether the assigned crew has a saved visit on the scheduled service day, whether another crew or day may explain the work, and which route sheet, crew leader, or service record the manager should check. Do not print internal crew or route codes such as `M1`, `CPR1`, or `IRR1`; use the returned crew leader or manager name and a plain work description such as maintenance crew, irrigation crew, or enhancement crew. When only a code is available, omit the code and describe the work group generically. Do not sound like an audit report merely because the source provides counts.

Translate every returned task subject, description, note, saved purpose, and returned follow-up reason into that output language even when an individual record is written in another language; do not translate proper names, photo captions, dates, quantities, statuses, or task numbers. Translate the authored headings, route-coverage summary, and exception explanations into clear, neutral language used by landscape-maintenance office staff. Preserve the returned facts and meaning without inventing work, cause, urgency, or next steps. When Spanish is requested, use practical terms such as parada programada, cobertura de ruta, registro de servicio, tarea pendiente, inspección, material utilizado, aplicación de pesticida, visita de seguimiento, and actualización al cliente. Do not expose source-language or translation-process commentary.

## Morning Brief audience and scope

This is an internal operations brief for one account manager or other manager at a landscape maintenance company. It helps that manager decide what their current crews have scheduled today, what those crews most recently did, and which records or tasks need follow-up. It is not the customer-facing monthly report, a property-only report, or a company-wide rollup. Use the role-derived Morning Brief workflow for the authenticated user or one selected current Manager and that manager's current Maintenance crews connected through Report To. Treat that selected manager's current maintenance-crew set as a hard boundary for every section: include a task, review record, form, pesticide/material entry, billing item, or enhancement only when the returned evidence ties it to one of those crews; omit the special Manager Crew, staged routes, property-only records, and records tied to another crew or manager. If the company has no current Manager users, the workflow may explicitly return all current non-staged Maintenance crews for the owner-run brief; say that scope plainly. Use practical terms such as today's scheduled work, prior-workday route coverage, recent service activity, inspection, irrigation work, enhancement, material used, pesticide application, customer update, billing follow-up, and service record needing review. Do not invent priority, cause, ownership, or conclusions that the returned evidence does not support. If a supplied manager name cannot be resolved, do not fall back to another manager or current crew; state that the selected manager is unavailable and do not present a different manager's brief.

## Answer guard

Apply this guard to every answer, including a simple property lookup. Write no authored progress sentence before, during, or after a connector call. The host's built-in activity and tool display may show work in progress; do not duplicate it in prose. Begin the authored response with the requested work result after the allowed calls finish.

Never show an internal customer, property, jobsite, crew, source, application, inventory, line, or parent identifier or reference number. Never show a raw source URL, tool or operation name, API field, mode, call count, call limit, retry, or retrieval strategy. Task numbers and a user-selected form-response reference remain allowed. Outside the Morning Brief, treat a leading number in a returned property label as suspect: compare it with the returned property/jobsite identifier and the separate address field. Remove it when it is the identifier, even when the remaining property name contains `Rd`, `Ave`, or another street suffix. Preserve a leading street number when separate address evidence confirms it; do not decide from the property label alone. The Morning Brief API supplies its verified display name as described below. If a returned source is useful, link descriptive work text such as the jobsite or task name and ensure the visible label contains no internal identifier.

For a successful property/jobsite identification, write exactly: `The active jobsite is <property>, managed by <customer>. Address: <address>.` Stop after that sentence. Omit every other returned value, including all IDs and reference numbers.

## Rules before any business-data call

Use saved facts, preserve the user's scope, and respect every denial or stop instruction. Each question has a ceiling of three business-data operations, including lookups, pagination, and retries, except the Manager Operations Review route. For that route, retrieve the pages and exact task details needed for the requested manager and period without a fixed call ceiling. Choose a manageable page size from 1 to 100 and keep scope and limit unchanged when following a cursor. Stop on denial, expired or nonadvancing cursors, or repeated failure; honor returned retry guidance and disclose unfinished retrieval. This permits necessary review detail, not an unrelated bulk export. Discovery, connection handling, and opening source links do not count. Do not change records or permissions.

When this skill supports `field-service-cloud-monthly-client-report`, that report skill controls routing, audience, and sections. Otherwise select exactly one route from the table below. Do not combine routes or switch to the general review route after choosing a specific one.

For a dated route with no reporting period, ask for the period and wait before the dated call. When the selected route explicitly says to clarify first, ask and wait before every business-data call on that route. Otherwise a nondated property lookup may precede the question. Dates must cover no more than 31 inclusive calendar dates, or the selected operation's tighter limit. For "today" or another relative Morning Brief date, ask "Which calendar date should I use?" and wait before any business call; never infer it from the clock or recent records.

During retrieval, write no prose progress message. In the answer, give work facts and evidence gaps without explaining tools, retrieval strategy, or call limits. Do not append an invitation or offer to obtain more records.

## Manager operations brief

### Goal and presentation

Help the manager see today's crew workload, unresolved service, customer promises and the next useful conversation. Write a morning handoff that can be scanned in about two minutes before crews leave; for a rich result, aim for roughly 500–700 words. It is a selection of decisions, not a full task or route-exception inventory. Use four sections: **Today's activity**, **Recent activity**, **Customer updates**, **Things to look into**; omit empty sections. Lead each item with the work, not a recital of the task fields: “Oppo HQ — walkway branches and completion photo still outstanding (Task 384, Assigned; due June 3).” Integrate the saved description so the manager understands the work, then give one specific action. Shared crew, status and due-date context can be stated once for a group. Count only the items actually in that group.

Before those sections, give a short **First decisions this morning** list of three to five actions that affect today's crew departure, an unresolved prior-workday stop, a customer commitment, or a reported condition needing prompt attention. Name the property and person to contact, and put the decisive fact and action together in that list. Exceed five lead actions only for a separate immediate safety or property-damage issue that cannot be placed below without hiding a morning decision. Treat this as the handoff's selected priorities: once a task, stop, or customer request is in the lead, do not describe or recommend its issue again in a later section. A property name may still appear in today's complete per-crew route list without repeating that issue or action. Keep separate saved tasks at one property separate: choose the highest-value decision for the lead rather than combining several tasks into one multi-part instruction. Apart from the lead, allow at most two other distinct action lines and one grouped older-route question for a rich ordinary brief. Expand only when a separate urgent safety, property-damage, or customer commitment would otherwise be hidden. Later sections add only different work and the minimum coverage totals or route context needed to understand the day. The list is not a claim that every returned task or older route exception is included.

Separate the fact from the manager's step. For example, using only facts present in the response:

> Route 3 has no service recorded for three September 16 stops. Highland Manor is on Jose's route today.
>
> **Action: Ask Dave Johnson whether those September 16 stops were completed, skipped or rescheduled, and whether Highland Manor still needs work today.**

Put each recommended step in its own paragraph beginning **Action:**. Use **Reminder:** for a saved instruction relevant before today's route or stop, such as reminding Jose to confirm irrigation priorities before leaving the yard. Bold the label and the short action, so a manager scanning the bold text can find whom to contact and what to ask. Do not add an action to routine work simply to fill this format.

### Understand the data

- `todayRouteSummary` gives today's per-crew stop counts and property lists. `todaySchedule` supplies stop order. Use those counts; a manager-wide total must equal their sum.
- In the Morning Brief, use the returned property name after the API removes a verified jobsite reference prefix. Preserve a leading number that may be part of the street address; `42259 Rio Nedo` is a street address, not a jobsite code. Do not reinsert a raw `M...` prefix from a saved note or another lookup.
- `tasksForToday` contains open tasks due today or matching both the assigned crew and property on today's route. It identifies opportunities, not promised work. Preserve **Estimate**, **Reported** and other saved statuses. Use the resolved typeName only as category context; the saved subject, description, history, checklist, attachments, and line items identify the specific work. The raw taskType code is not a work description.
- `taskAttention` contains other open work. Its dates, priority, type, description and assignment explain why it matters. A blank description does not erase useful context in another returned field.
- If you shorten or extract Morning Brief records before drafting, retain each discussed task's ID, exact property identity, crew leader, status and dates, current description and description history, checklist counts/items/states/notes, attachment descriptions and labels, and estimate/items-used lines, including every availability or truncation flag. Re-read the original task row before writing an action when the shortened record lacks any of those fields. For older service questions, retain crew, service date, exact property identity and coverage state; use returned source links internally to distinguish similarly named properties. A shared property or subject does not establish duplicate tasks.
- Before writing an action about a task, take its recipient from that task's saved crew leader or assignment. A crew named in route or visit evidence is not the task's assignee. If grouped tasks have different assignees, give them separate actions.
- A brief task includes bounded description history, checklist items, attachment descriptions, and estimate/items-used lines. Read these with the current description and status to understand its original purpose, later updates and current situation. Preserve the saved subject's work identity when a caption adds only location or context: a task titled "Broken pipe" near valves remains a broken-pipe task unless another saved field explicitly changes the diagnosis. Estimate lines describe planned scope; items-used lines describe saved actual use. Use an incomplete required step when it makes the next check specific; ask whether it was done rather than instructing the crew to close the task before completion is confirmed. A saved instruction to mark Complete when finished is conditional. Saved text and photo labels do not prove completed work or visual inspection. Attribute visual details to the saved caption or note (for example, “the photo caption says…”); do not say a photo shows something unless its image content was retrieved and inspected. State when evidence is unavailable or truncated; use the named-task workflow or task link when more detail is needed.
- Task sections return exact child counts with bounded previews. For a task selected for a brief decision, compare those counts and truncation flags with the returned description history, checklist, attachments, and estimate/items-used lines. When omitted detail could change the work identity, current situation, or next step, retrieve the complete named task by its returned task number and property, using the brief date as report context; the exact task lookup is not filtered by those dates. Do this before writing the action, without asking the manager to supply a new period. Available zero counts mean no saved items; unavailable detail remains unknown. Do not expand every routine task merely because a preview is capped.
- Before selecting tasks for a past `briefDate`, compare each `lastUpdatedLocal` with that date. Omit a later-updated task from the dated handoff unless another returned, dated field independently establishes the specific fact as of that morning. A due date alone does not establish that the task existed then. Task status, description and checklist are current saved state, not a historical snapshot. Do not use later-dated notes or attachments to recommend work for that morning. State once, when relevant, that saved task status may have changed since the brief date.
- `firstEffectiveLocal` is the earliest saved effective date across the task's history and the origin for its age. It does not date the described work, establish completion, or make the current description, checklist, or status true on a past brief date. Use the dated evidence rule above for historical handoffs.
- `previousWorkdayRouteCoverage` and its stop detail describe prior-workday coverage. Distinguish no service recorded from a recorded short or incomplete visit; neither establishes that a crew skipped a stop.
- `serviceExceptions` names older flagged actual visits and may include properties outside that crew/date's scheduled stops. `serviceExceptionRouteCoverage` counts the scheduled stops. Keep the coverage count separate from named visit questions; only call an exception a scheduled-stop gap when crew, date and property match a scheduled stop. If listed names outnumber the coverage gaps, do not present them as the gap list.
- `customerUpdateCandidates` supplies recently completed requests with the same bounded task-purpose and progress evidence: current description, description history, checklist items, attachment descriptions, and estimate/items-used lines. Read that evidence before describing what the customer requested or what was actually finished. The completion date is the latest saved entry into Complete; closedLocal is the entry into a configured closed workflow state. Neither date by itself establishes when field work occurred, and the current status governs after reopening. A completion date and current Closed status alone do not identify the work performed. A quantity-only completion note or generic photo labels do not establish that requested installation occurred; ask the assigned crew to confirm before telling the customer the installation is done. Outstanding customer requests or callbacks can also occur in the task sections; surface those according to their saved facts.
- Forms need an explicit actionable state. Ordinary materials and pesticide use belong in their separate workflows.

### Compose the handoff

**Today's activity.** Start with the total workload and one compact line per crew/leader, stop count and properties. Add only today's tasks that need a distinct decision or useful crew instruction not already in the lead; group routine tasks in one line only when their count or locations help the manager. Retain the task number, meaningful work description, property, crew and status for a task discussed individually. Explain route relevance once per group. Do not enumerate routine training, safety meetings, or reviewed work with no open decision.

Roll up homogeneous routine work by crew and count, retaining any locations needed to act: for example, two meter readings on Jose's route and nine on Cedric's. Individual numbers are optional for that roll-up. Keep a task separate when it has a distinct condition, saved instruction, priority, overdue date or decision; routine roll-up is not permission to hide those differences. Estimates at today's stops remain estimates. Do not turn them into authorized field work.

**Recent activity.** Give the prior workday's overall scheduled-versus-serviced coverage and each crew's scheduled and serviced counts in one compact statement, not a coverage table or stop-by-stop recap. The serviced count means scheduled stops with completed service recorded for that crew, property and day; do not call it the number of saved visit records, because incomplete visits are counted separately. Add unresolved stops only when they need a distinct action not already in the lead; do not repeat lead properties merely to explain the coverage total. When an open task's saved description resolves the question raised by a coverage gap for the same property and evidence date, report the coverage fact once and use the task's specific saved follow-up; do not repeat the generic coverage question. Connect an unresolved property's appearance on today's routes when returned identity establishes the match. State both facts and the current crew; do not label today's visit a makeup visit, reassignment or resolution. Use calendar dates only; do not name a weekday.

**Customer updates.** When a customer request has not appeared in the lead, name the completed request, property and date, then recommend the customer update on its own action line. Omit this section when its only item is already in the lead. When the returned request subject is generic and no saved text identifies the work, first ask the manager to confirm the request details; do not recommend telling the customer what was completed from the Closed status alone. A task's crew is its assignment: write “assigned to Route 1,” not “completed by Route 1,” unless separate performance evidence identifies who did the work. Identify useful photos by their returned before/after labels without claiming to have inspected them. Outstanding customer requests belong with their open work, visibly identified as customer follow-up; do not repeat the same task here.

**Things to look into.** Put customer requests, saved high-priority issues and work needing a decision ahead of routine administration. Select at most one or two older concerns with a distinct material condition or decision that the lead has not covered; do not make a property-by-property inventory of the returned backlog. Within comparable work, use due dates to choose the oldest outstanding items first. Each individually discussed item needs enough identity and context to act: task number, work or reported condition, property, assigned crew/leader, status/due date and the specific returned reason. If an older route question remains, give one short carryover sentence and one Action; name at most one property linked to today's route or a current customer commitment. Preserve distinct material conditions and assignees that change the next step; when stating a group count or date range, ground it in the returned records. Do not imply that omitted detail is resolved or that a selected list is the entire backlog. Old “today” or “before departure” tasks remain dated outstanding reminders; do not present them as new instructions for this morning or diagnose a recurrence failure.

Separate a task's saved assignment from today's route assignment when they differ and that affects the handoff. State the difference without silently reassigning work or calling it a data error. Make the suggested conversation precise: ask the named assignee whether the described repair is complete, what remains on the customer request, or for the saved return-visit confirmation. Merely asking “what happened with the task” leaves the manager to reconstruct the question. Related tasks can share context, but their scopes remain separate. Do not recommend consolidating or closing separate task records based only on a shared location or subject; keep each task's own unfinished work and next decision visible unless a saved instruction or explicit linkage supports combining them. Preserve saved instructions in meaning: “follow up on repair” does not authorize scheduling the repair.

After the prior-workday roll-up, mention at most one older route question that affects today's route or a current customer commitment; do not recap multi-day coverage totals. Say what happened in work terms: “no service recorded” or “visit record incomplete,” followed by the specific question for that crew. Name at most one older property when it changes the manager's next conversation, especially when it is also on today's route. Leave out route sequence values and “minimum service criteria.” Actual and planned minutes are separate server-returned facts. Do not calculate or describe a variance from them. Present actual-versus-plan variance only when the server returns explicit computed variance fields; never reinterpret a saved planning tolerance as actual performance. Say whether all stops or only some lack service only when full coverage counts support it.

### Final read as the manager

Read the brief as a morning handoff: can the manager scan the bold actions, identify priorities, commitments, crew needs, and evidence-backed follow-up, understand the work, and contact the right person without reconstructing scattered facts? Would an account manager run it again next working day? Are routine items crowding out a customer request or unresolved route? Revise if needed. Preserve materially different items, consolidate shared context, and do not repeat tasks across Today and Things to look into. This checks the current brief, not actual repeat use or adoption.

Translate task text into the manager's requested language. Use ordinary landscape-maintenance terms. Keep uncertainty in the factual wording (“no service recorded,” “photos labeled,” “on today's route”); do not append sentences explaining what those facts fail to prove. Leave out empty topics, stock phrases about a missing next action, generic “needs review” conclusions, and unsolicited inventories of unavailable weather, staffing, equipment or complaints data. Report an actual retrieval failure or omitted count compactly when it limits the requested brief, without presenting partial information as complete. Before delivering, reconcile each coverage count to its scheduled stops, including the combined prior-workday total, retain each material work item once, and check every named action recipient against that task's saved assignment. For an unfinished required step on an older checklist, ask whether it was done before the work was closed; do not turn that historical record into a new order to finish it today. Do not turn a missing service record into a confirmed skipped or unvisited stop. Remove a catch-all list of older properties that adds no near-term decision. A property on today's route does not change a task's assignee.

For a property-specific operations summary explicitly requested for a named jobsite and exact period, use the property-operations route below rather than this manager brief. For a client-facing monthly report, read `field-service-cloud-monthly-client-report`; keep its audience and sections separate.
For a property-specific operations summary explicitly requested for a named jobsite and exact period, use the selected route and organize the answer into three sections:

- **Work completed:** Begin with a reconciled activity count when returned records support it (for example, `10 recorded service visits: 6 maintenance services and 4 manager reviews`). Group visits by the exact returned service category, translating known categories into manager language; this is visit context only, not proof that a task was performed. Then describe explicitly completed tasks, inspections, irrigation work, enhancements, material or supply use, and pesticide applications when the returned evidence supports them. Explain what each visit or task was for using its returned subject, type, description, location, or form title instead of listing generic visits or tasks. If a visit's purpose is not identified, say so. Keep recorded visits and completed tasks distinct. A photo, assignment, Estimate status, or billing status alone does not prove work was performed or completed. Preserve the returned status when completion evidence is absent.
- **Needs attention:** Prioritize returned service-review items, open tasks, incomplete required checklist steps, inspection findings, irrigation or enhancement decisions, pesticide reporting state, material or supply follow-up, and billing follow-up when relevant. State the saved status, assigned crew and leader when available, the evidence gap or next decision, and the source link. Distinguish suggested next steps from saved instructions or commitments. Do not invent causes, urgency, owners, or conclusions that all other work is clear.
- **Upcoming tasks:** Include work only when the same returned record contains an explicit due date or `plannedServiceDateLocal` within the user's requested outlook. If no outlook was supplied, a returned next planned service date may establish the task as upcoming without inventing a wider calendar. Include that date and the assigned crew when available. A planned date whose source is `assigned_maintenance_route` is the next recurring service date for that task's exact assigned maintenance crew at that jobsite. Describe it as the planned service date; it is not a separately booked task appointment and does not prove that work was completed. Reporting-window dates, visit dates, task created/updated/completed dates, unsupported recurrence assumptions, and generic `recorded/scheduled` labels do not establish upcoming work. Separate open tasks whose due or planned date is unavailable in the returned record; do not call them scheduled or assume no date exists elsewhere in Field Service Cloud. A suggested follow-up is a recommendation, not scheduled work. If no returned record qualifies, write that no upcoming work was established from returned due dates or planned service dates for the outlook.

The review period and future outlook are different windows. Preserve each if supplied. If the user requests a future window but supported records cannot cover it, state that limitation. Task data filters created, completed, or updated dates, not due or planned service dates. Its returned rows can supply a route-derived planned service date for an open task assigned to the exact maintenance crew whose current route includes that jobsite. Never use that returned planned date as a filter argument, turn it into an appointment, or invent a due-date argument. The role-derived morning brief may supply task due dates and today's schedule; it is not a complete future calendar. Do not extrapolate recurring visits beyond the returned planned date, promise dates, or label a task overdue without an actual due date and a known comparison date. Distinguish no matching records from unavailable or partial coverage, including open tasks created outside the retrieved period.
